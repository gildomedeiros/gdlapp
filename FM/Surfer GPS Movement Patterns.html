Surfer GPS movement patterns

VT field analysis · 5–6 November 2026 · Brisbane time

What the logs show

Use direction persistence as well as speed. Tight clusters and frequent changes of direction favour holding aim. Steady movement is a better candidate for a short camera lead.

The sample includes 18,731 accepted GPS receipts and 1,762 movement windows. Sitting, drifting, paddling and riding are possible explanations for the patterns. Confirming those behaviours requires matching the windows to footage.

During a logged ride

All six patterns appeared while VT flagged a ride. Steady directed movement was the most common group among windows where the flag stayed on throughout. Fast directed and sustained ride-speed movement were much more likely to overlap the flag than holding position.

These counts use the VT movement_cycle.riding flag from the same log. Windows needed ride-state updates within one second of both ends and no internal update gap over one second. Three holding windows lacked sufficient coverage. The flag can stay on during slower movement, and its agreement with actual wave riding still needs video confirmation.

FM replay evidence. The 0511 replay used GPS stretches over 10 km/h. Both versions agreed with the bearing trend on 65 of 65 targets in steady stretches. The revised Rule 1 prevented the false reversal in a separate 18 km/h test with an injected GPS jump. The group descriptions explain expected publication behavior. Actual pan and camera framing depend on the mount and were outside the GPS replay.

The one-metre sitting hypothesis. In 707 of the 801 holding windows, the total spread on each axis was at most one logged coordinate step, about 1.1 m north–south and 1.0 m east–west. This is the full extent of the cluster along each axis. A single GPS step offers little evidence of movement. Staying in a few cells over several seconds is a stronger signal.

The 12 km/h ride hypothesis. There were 742 consecutive-fix readings at or above 12 km/h, using packet intervals of 0.3–0.8 seconds. Of those, 220 also reached 12 km/h using net displacement over the preceding roughly two seconds. These are readings, so multiple hits can belong to the same event. The counts do not measure ride detection accuracy.

How well did a short lead work?

For each window, I predicted the surfer’s GPS position roughly two seconds ahead. One prediction held the last fix. The other extended the window’s average velocity, including its direction. Lower median error is better.

Candidate rules for a VT replay test

  1. Hold through clusters and direction changes. Treat a single positional jump cautiously. Try holding a stable cluster centre and compare it with the last-fix baseline above.
  2. Add lead when movement persists. Start testing in the 4–8 km/h directed group, where extrapolation reduced median error. Check whether a shorter lead gives a better balance near turns.
  3. Check a fast burst over a second time scale. Look for consistent direction and sustained speed around 12 km/h across roughly two seconds. Use footage to test how early that identifies takeoff, how often paddling gets mistaken for a ride, and what happens at ride end.

These are replay candidates. The 2.25 m spread, 55% consistency and speed bands are exploratory cutoffs. The five-second window describes the groups and can delay detection of a short takeoff.

The comparison uses the first receipt at least seven seconds after the same start, within 7.8 seconds. Excluded windows with receipt gaps over 1.25 seconds through that comparison point.
  • Spread is the largest pairwise distance between fixes. Direction consistency is net displacement divided by the sum of successive step distances. Prediction errors compare projected positions with later GPS fixes.
  • Latitudes and longitudes were converted to approximate local metres. Reported results describe recorded GPS, with its coordinate rounding and receipt timing. Movement labels were inferred from GPS geometry and were not independently video-labelled.
